What Are Writing Statistics?

Writing statistics are quantitative measurements that describe the structural properties of a text. While basic statistics like word count and character count tell you how long your text is, advanced writing statistics tell you how readable, complex, and sophisticated your text is. They answer questions like: "Can an eighth-grader understand this?" and "Is my sentence structure varied enough?"

Understanding Readability: The Flesch-Kincaid Score

The Flesch-Kincaid readability tests are the most widely used readability formulas in the world, developed in 1975 by J. Peter Kincaid under contract to the U.S. Navy. They consist of two related scores: the Flesch Reading Ease score and the Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level.

Flesch Reading Ease

The Flesch Reading Ease score ranges from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate easier readability:

Reading Ease = 206.835 − (1.015 × ASL) − (84.6 × ASW)

Where ASL = Average Sentence Length (words per sentence) and ASW = Average Syllables per Word.

80–89Easy6th-grade students; consumer-facing content
70–79Fairly Easy7th-grade students; good for general web content
60–69Standard8th–9th grade; most online articles and blogs
50–59Fairly Difficult10th–12th grade; more complex topics
30–49DifficultCollege level; academic and technical writing

Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level

The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level converts the Reading Ease score into a U.S. school grade level. For example, a score of 8.0 means the text should be understandable to an average 8th-grade student.

Grade Level = (0.39 × ASL) + (11.8 × ASW) − 15.59
📊 Target grade levels by content type: General web content: 7–9. News articles: 8–10. Blog posts: 7–9. Academic papers: 12+. Legal documents: often 15+ (though many advocates push for simpler legal writing). Technical documentation: 10–14. Children's books: 1–5.

Sources: Kincaid, J.P., et al. (1975). "Derivation of New Readability Formulas." U.S. Navy Technical Report. See also: Readable.com Flesch-Kincaid Guide.

How Syllable Counting Works — And Why It Matters

Syllable count is a key input to readability formulas. A syllable is a unit of pronunciation that contains a vowel sound. For example, "read" has 1 syllable, "reading" has 2, and "readability" has 5. Words with more syllables are generally more complex and contribute to higher (more difficult) readability scores.

Syllable Counting Rules (Simplified)

  • Count the number of vowel sounds (a, e, i, o, u, and sometimes y) in a word
  • Silent 'e' at the end of words does not count as a syllable (e.g., "cake" = 1 syllable)
  • Two vowels together often form one syllable (e.g., "read" = 1 syllable), but not always (e.g., "create" = 2 syllables)
  • Words ending in "-le" after a consonant add a syllable (e.g., "table" = 2 syllables: ta-ble)
💡 Did you know? The average English word has about 1.4 syllables. If your text's average syllables per word is above 1.7, your content may feel dense or academic. If it is below 1.2, your content may feel simplistic. Most high-performing blog content sits between 1.3 and 1.6 syllables per word.

Average Word Length and Sentence Length: What the Numbers Mean

Average Word Length

Average word length is calculated by dividing the total number of characters (excluding spaces) by the total number of words. The typical range for English text is 4.5 to 5.5 characters per word. Shorter average word length generally correlates with easier readability — but not always. Technical writing often uses shorter words (like "API," "CPU," "SQL") that are nevertheless specialized.

Average Sentence Length

Average sentence length is one of the strongest predictors of readability. Research shows that:

  • 15–20 words per sentence: Optimal for general audiences and web content
  • 20–25 words per sentence: Acceptable for educated readers and business writing
  • 25–35 words per sentence: Becoming dense; mix in shorter sentences for variety
  • 35+ words per sentence: Difficult; readers may lose the thread before reaching the end

Variety matters more than the average. A paragraph with five 18-word sentences feels monotonous; a paragraph that alternates between 5-word and 25-word sentences feels dynamic and engaging.

📊 Industry benchmarks: The New York Times averages about 20 words per sentence. Hemingway averaged about 16 words per sentence. Academic journals average 30–35 words per sentence. Legal contracts can exceed 50 words per sentence — which is why they are notoriously difficult to understand.

Source: DuBay, W.H. (2004). "The Principles of Readability." Impact Information. See also: Readability (Wikipedia).

Lexical Diversity: How Rich Is Your Vocabulary?

Lexical diversity measures the range of different words used in a text. It is calculated as the ratio of unique words to total words. A text with high lexical diversity uses many different words; a text with low lexical diversity repeats the same words frequently.

Lexical Diversity = Unique Words ÷ Total Words

Interpretation guide:

Lexical DiversityInterpretation
Above 0.70Very rich vocabulary; typical of literary fiction and academic writing
0.55 – 0.70Good variety; typical of well-written blog posts and articles
0.40 – 0.55Moderate; common in instructional content and how-to guides
Below 0.40Repetitive; may indicate keyword stuffing or limited vocabulary
⚠️ Note: Lexical diversity is sensitive to text length. Short texts (under 100 words) naturally have higher diversity ratios because there is less opportunity for repetition. For the most meaningful results, analyze texts of at least 300–500 words.

How to Use Writing Statistics to Improve Your Writing

1

Identify your target audience and reading level

Before analyzing your text, decide who you are writing for. A blog post for general readers should aim for a Flesch-Kincaid grade level of 7–9. An academic paper for a peer-reviewed journal can target grade 12+. A children's story should target grade 1–4.

2

Paste your draft and review the metrics

Copy your text into the analyzer above and click Analyze. Review your Flesch-Kincaid grade level, average sentence length, and lexical diversity. Compare them against the benchmarks for your content type.

3

Adjust based on insights

If your grade level is too high, shorten long sentences and replace complex words with simpler alternatives. If your lexical diversity is low (below 0.40), vary your word choice — use synonyms and avoid repeating the same terms excessively. If your average sentence length is above 30, break up some long sentences.

4

Re-analyze and iterate

After each round of edits, paste your revised text back into the analyzer. Good writing is rewriting — use the statistics to guide your revisions, not to judge your first draft.

Frequently Asked Questions About Writing Statistics

What is a good Flesch-Kincaid grade level?

For most web content, a grade level of 7–9 is ideal — it means your content is accessible to the majority of adult readers. For academic or technical writing, grade levels of 12+ are expected. Use our writing statistics tool to check your score.

How accurate is the Flesch-Kincaid score?

The Flesch-Kincaid formula correlates strongly with actual reading difficulty across large samples of text. However, it is a statistical model — it does not account for conceptual complexity, prior knowledge required, or visual presentation. Use it as a helpful guide, not an absolute measure.

What is lexical diversity?

Lexical diversity, also called type-token ratio (TTR), measures how many unique words appear in your text relative to the total word count. A high ratio means you use a wide variety of words. Check your lexical diversity with our word counter tool.

Why does average sentence length matter?

Sentence length is one of the strongest predictors of readability. Long sentences strain working memory and increase the risk that readers will lose track of the main point. Varying sentence length — mixing short punchy sentences with longer, more complex ones — creates rhythm and keeps readers engaged.
